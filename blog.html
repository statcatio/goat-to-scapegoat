Field Notes · No. 01 From GOAT to Scapegoat · behind the numbers

Measuring the internet’s turn on Argentina

How the sentiment numbers behind From GOAT to Scapegoat were actually collected and scored — where the data came from, which model did the scoring, and what I checked before trusting the result enough to publish it.

Background

I started watching soccer around 2008, during the Messi/Ronaldo era at Barcelona and Real Madrid. Messi wasn’t “the GOAT” yet, that conversation was still open. I stopped following the sport closely after college, so by the time this year’s World Cup happened I was mostly watching from the outside.

What got my attention wasn’t the games, it was the comments. People calling Argentina FIFA’s favorite team, accusing refs of favoring them, calling the players obnoxious. This is the same team that won in 2022 to near universal praise. So I wanted to actually check: did the public’s opinion of Argentina change, or did Argentina change?

In this post I’m going to look at:

  • Did sentiment toward Argentina actually fall between the 2022 and 2026 finals, or is that just a feeling from a loud subset of comments?
  • If it fell, is that just because they lost this time (losers get talked about worse than winners, that’s not news), or is something else going on?
  • Does the pattern hold across more than one audience, or is it specific to one comment section?

Experimental setup

Data collection

My first plan was Reddit match threads, since they’re timestamped during the match. That fell through because Reddit closed self-service Data API access under its Responsible Builder Policy, which covers Reddit data however it’s obtained, including scraped mirrors. So I moved to YouTube comments under the official highlights of each final, pulled through the YouTube Data API v3.

I used two channels per final, FOX and FIFA, so the result would have to hold up across two separate audiences rather than one — that’s 4 videos total (2 finals × 2 channels). I collected 45,016 comments and kept 28,051 for scoring, for two reasons:

  1. I only kept top-level comments. The API caps inline replies at 5 per thread, so replies came back only about 40% complete, and mixing a fully-captured sample with a partially-captured one would bias the comparison.
  2. I restricted to the first 42 days after each video posted. 2022’s videos have years of accumulated comments and 2026’s have weeks, so comparing raw totals would be unfair — 42 days made the two finals comparable over equal elapsed time.

Model selection

For sentiment scoring I used cardiffnlp/twitter-xlm-roberta-base-sentiment, a multilingual model. I picked multilingual specifically because a large share of the comments are in Spanish, and most off-the-shelf sentiment models are English-only. An English-only model scores non-English text as roughly neutral by default, which would have quietly dropped a big chunk of Argentina’s own fans out of the data and made the sentiment drop look bigger than it actually is.

For attributing a comment to a team (is this comment about Argentina or about the opponent), I used keyword matching on team and player names rather than a model, since I wanted that step to be checkable. The keyword lists are published alongside the data in data/public/aggregates.json.

Pipeline

The pipeline is two scripts. scrape_youtube.py pulls comments via the API and writes raw data locally (not committed, since it’s full of usernames and comment text from people who never agreed to be published). analyze.py filters to top-level comments within the 42-day window, runs sentiment scoring, attributes each comment by keyword, and aggregates into the public numbers (counts, means, keyword lists — no usernames or raw text).

Checking the result

Before trusting the headline number I ran two checks against it:

Check 01

Is this just “losers get hated on”?

Argentina won in 2022 and lost in 2026, so some drop is expected from the result alone. To isolate that, I needed a team that also lost a final, as a control. France lost the 2022 final and stayed net positive the whole time (+0.14 on FOX, +0.21 on FIFA). So losing a final doesn’t do this to a team by default — something about Argentina specifically is different.

Check 02

Is the model picking up anger aimed at the opponent instead?

A comment like “Spain only won because the ref cheated” is hostile in tone but pro-Argentina, and it names Argentina, so it counts toward Argentina’s score. I checked what happens when comments naming both teams are excluded. The gap holds.

Findings

The same flip appears in FIFA’s audience: +0.42 in 2022 to −0.17 in 2026. Negative comments about Argentina went from roughly 1-in-5 to over half.

France, the control, lost their 2022 final and still finished net positive. Argentina lost their 2026 final and went net negative — further than the loss alone explains — and that gap shows up independently in both audiences.

Limits

  • Sarcasm still breaks sentiment models, and this one is no exception. I couldn’t correct for it, only flag it.
  • France isn’t a perfect control — Spain’s fanbase isn’t France’s fanbase.
  • Conduct- and tactics-specific subtopics rest on small samples (n = 48–99) and are labelled as such.
  • The YouTube API consistently returned about 75% of each video’s reported comment count, across all four videos, so this measures the discourse about Argentina that was retrievable, not a full census.

A note on tools

This was a weekend project. I used Claude Code as a pair programmer to build both scripts above (the scraper and the analysis/scoring pipeline) and to work through the two robustness checks before I trusted the result enough to publish it. The framing, the research questions, and the conclusions are mine.
